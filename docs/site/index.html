A new kind of intelligence test

Can AI systems recognize intelligence in one another?

Most AI evaluations begin with human-written questions. This experiment turns the process around: capable models invent the questions, interrogate anonymous peers, and decide for themselves what counts as convincing evidence of intelligence.

Independent replication 86.4%

of comparable model pairs were ordered consistently with an external intelligence index, averaged across two independent 10-probe judges.

Why build this?

Benchmarks are good at measuring tasks with verifiable answers: factual recall, mathematics, coding, exams, and agentic work. They are less direct measures of qualities people also associate with intelligence: judgment, creativity, strategic thought, adaptability, social reasoning, and the ability to recognize competence in someone else.

Models already evaluate model outputs, but usually against human-written rubrics. Here the evaluation method is itself the object of study. What do models test when nobody defines intelligence for them? Do they ask better questions as evidence accumulates? Can a weaker system recognize a stronger one?

Method in 30 seconds

Anonymous candidates, independent judges

Two frontier judges independently wrote 10 opening probes and sent each probe unchanged to 50 anonymous models. Each judge compared all answers to a probe, combined the evidence into a ranking, and could ask two later follow-ups of the ten candidates it found hardest to separate. Candidate names and external scores were hidden throughout. Earlier four- and five-probe runs and crossed-evidence controls provide stability checks.

50candidate models
2independent judges
10 + 2opening + adaptive probes
47direct external scores

The external Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index is a useful reference, not a literal ground truth. Exact model settings can differ, nearby scores are noisy, and three deliberately weak anchors use estimates. See the model table, the 12-strategy / 27-question taxonomy, and the small human audit sample.

Three complementary tests

From a broad ladder to above-level oversight

The experiments reuse one basic idea at different scales: hide model identities, let a judge invent the evidence it wants, and compare its resulting order with an external reference.

Research question 2

Can a judge recognize a model more capable than itself?

Often, but not reliably. Across 30 panels and 10 judges, 77 of 118 stronger candidates were placed above the judge's anonymous self.

65.3%stronger candidates recognized
92.9%recognized at 10+ point lead
48.0%lower-third judges, standardized
85.8%upper-third judges, standardized
What this does and does not show. After standardizing the three sub-ten-point margin bins, recognition rises from 48.0% for lower-third judges to 85.8% for upper-third judges. Their panel-bootstrap intervals are 25.5%-69.7% and 68.6%-96.9%. This suggests judge capability matters, but judge identity, provider, probe battery, and panel are still entangled. Candidate leads below ten points do not form a clean monotonic threshold.

Research question 3

Do stronger models ask different questions?

Yes, descriptively, although not in the simple sense that every question is better. Across 161 probes from 11 authors, the four higher-capability authors concentrated heavily on quantitative stress tests. Lower-capability authors spread more attention across verbal and fluid abstraction, creative generation, and criteria-setting. Rates first average within author, so a model that wrote 23 probes does not outweigh one that wrote six.

88%reference-valid, higher band
61%reference-valid, lower band
82%objective, higher band
30%objective, lower band
The careful reading. Stronger authors produced more checkable, reference-valid batteries in this sample, but author intelligence had only a weak relationship with a probe's eventual pairwise discrimination. Author, provider, panel, and prompt history are observationally entangled. This is a map of an emerging repertoire, not a causal claim that capability alone creates a particular test style.

Opening batteries usually broadened: 46 of 72 transitions changed capability area. Adaptive follow-ups split between 6 broadening, 7 deepening, and 5 generic continuations. Across the oversight panels, follow-ups improved ten rankings, left eleven unchanged, and worsened nine.

The useful negative result. Question-type predictions reached 50.0% author-level concordance; the complete label set reached 52.4%. Both intervals include chance. The taxonomy describes evaluation behavior; it is not yet a validated recipe for choosing questions. A single blind author attempt also cannot establish that a probe was truly beyond the author's capability.

Research question 4 · mechanism pilot

Can probe design or independent councils improve judgment?

Neither intervention is a universal fix. Across four matched nine-model panels, verifier-oriented instructions changed what judges asked but moved above-self recognition from 60.0% to 55.0%. Three independent evaluators improved on Sol alone for the ordinary batteries, from 64.6% to 70.1% pairwise accuracy.

60.0%ordinary above-self recognition
55.0%verifier above-self recognition
70.1%ordinary council accuracy
75.0%verifier council accuracy
Why keep both outcomes? A battery can improve the broad ordering while making the judge less likely to put every externally stronger model above itself. Four panels are enough to diagnose mechanisms and failures, not to estimate a universal effect. Evaluator disagreement, external-index noise, and model-family composition remain material. The combined interventions showed no extra benefit: the council gain was smaller with verifier batteries, an interaction of -2.8%.
